## Baroque

**Period:** Around 1600 to 1750. It started in Rome and spread to Spain, Flanders and the Dutch Republic.

**Story:** Baroque started in Rome around 1600. After the Reformation, the Catholic Church wanted art that would move people, so painters went for drama: strong emotions, bodies in motion, and bright light coming out of dark backgrounds. Caravaggio made this contrast famous and a lot of painters copied him. In Flanders, Rubens painted huge scenes full of energy. In the Dutch Republic, Rembrandt and Vermeer used the same kind of light for portraits and quiet everyday scenes, and in Spain, Velázquez painted the royal court. The style lasted until around 1750.

**What to look for:** Dark backgrounds with a strong light on the main figures. Warm browns, reds and gold. Diagonal compositions that look like they are moving. Dramatic moments from the Bible or mythology, but also still lifes with food and silverware.

**Famous example:** The Night Watch, Rembrandt, 1642 (Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam)

## Cubism

**Period:** About 1907 to the 1920s, in Paris.

**Story:** Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque started Cubism in Paris around 1907 and 1908. Instead of painting an object from one point of view, they broke it into flat geometric pieces and showed several sides of it at once, a bit like walking around it. A critic said Braque's paintings were made of little cubes, and the name stuck. The early Cubist paintings are mostly brown and grey, so you focus on the shapes. Later they started gluing paper and newspaper onto the canvas (collage) and used brighter colours. Juan Gris and Fernand Léger then took the style in their own direction.

**What to look for:** Objects and faces broken into flat, angular pieces. Several points of view mixed in the same image. Not much depth, everything seems pushed to the front. Guitars, bottles, newspapers and café tables come up a lot. Early works are brown and grey, later ones more colourful.

**Famous example:** Les Demoiselles d'Avignon, Pablo Picasso, 1907 (MoMA, New York), often seen as the starting point of Cubism

## Impressionism

**Period:** 1860s to 1880s, in France.

**Story:** In the 1860s, a group of young painters in Paris got tired of the official Salon, which mostly wanted polished history paintings. Monet, Renoir, Pissarro, Degas, Berthe Morisot and others painted modern life and landscapes instead, often outdoors and quickly, to catch the light at a precise moment. In 1874 they organised their own exhibition. A critic made fun of Monet's painting Impression, Sunrise and called the group "Impressionists", and they kept the name. At first people found the paintings messy and unfinished. Today it's one of the most popular movements, and it opened the way to modern art.

**What to look for:** Short brushstrokes you can see. Light and weather are the real subject: sun on water, fog, snow. Bright colours, even in the shadows, which are often blue or purple instead of black. Outdoor scenes, boats, gardens, cafés. Up close it looks unfinished, but from a few steps back it works.

**Famous example:** Impression, Sunrise, Claude Monet, 1872 (Musée Marmottan Monet, Paris)

## Pop_Art

**Period:** Mid-1950s to the 1970s, in Britain and the United States.

**Story:** Pop Art started in Britain in the mid-1950s and really took off in New York in the 1960s. Artists took images from everyday mass culture, like adverts, comics, supermarket products and film stars, and turned them into art. Andy Warhol printed rows of soup cans and portraits of Marilyn Monroe, Roy Lichtenstein painted giant comic-strip panels with the printing dots visible, and Claes Oldenburg made huge soft sculptures of burgers and ice creams. It was partly a reaction against abstract art, which felt serious and hard to get into. Some people saw Pop Art as a celebration of consumer culture, others as a critique of it.

**What to look for:** Bright flat colours and hard black outlines. Comic-book dots. The same image repeated several times. Brands, food, celebrities and cartoons. Sometimes it isn't a painting at all but a print, or a giant everyday object.

**Famous example:** Campbell's Soup Cans, Andy Warhol, 1962 (MoMA, New York)

## Ukiyo_e

**Period:** 17th to 19th century, in Japan (Edo period).

**Story:** Ukiyo-e means "pictures of the floating world". It is a kind of Japanese art, mostly woodblock prints, made in Edo (today's Tokyo) between the 17th and 19th centuries. The "floating world" was the city's entertainment life: kabuki theatre, actors, teahouses and courtesans. Because the prints were made from carved wooden blocks, they could be produced in large numbers and sold cheaply, so ordinary people could buy them. Later, artists like Hokusai and Hiroshige made famous series of landscapes. When the prints reached Europe in the 19th century, they had a big influence on Monet, Van Gogh and other painters.

**What to look for:** Strong outlines and flat areas of colour with very little shading. Beige or off-white paper. Japanese writing and small red seals in the corners. Kabuki actors, women in kimonos, waves, Mount Fuji, bridges in the rain.

**Famous example:** The Great Wave off Kanagawa, Katsushika Hokusai, around 1831
